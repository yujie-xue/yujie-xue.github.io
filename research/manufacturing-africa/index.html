Wages, Productive Capabilities, and Manufacturing Adjustment in Africa

Industry Comovement and Production-Side Policy Adjustment

Gamal Eltaib Ibrahim · Yujie Xue · Abay Agidew Zeleke

Research summary

Are low wages enough to support industrial development? This paper brings together two levels of evidence: an African manufacturing panel and a study of production-side adjustment around the introduction of Mauritius’s national minimum wage in 2018.

Panel coverage
23 African countries
Period
2006–2022
Policy episode
Mauritius, 2018

The UNIDO panel covers 24 manufacturing divisions. The principal fixed-effects estimates use seven countries. Higher average wages are positively associated with value added and output, while the estimates for employment and establishment counts are not statistically distinguishable from zero. These are conditional associations, rather than estimates of the causal effect of increasing wages.

The Mauritius analysis finds no precisely estimated differential change in employment or establishments through 2019, but the confidence intervals allow economically substantial effects. Administrative evidence shows contraction concentrated in export-oriented apparel manufacturing. It also illustrates how measured labour productivity can rise when labour input falls faster than output.

Together, the two layers highlight an important distinction: wage–performance comovement can be consistent with stronger productive capabilities, while an increase in measured productivity can also reflect contraction and reallocation. Neither low wages as a sufficient industrial strategy nor a higher wage floor as a costless route to upgrading is established by this evidence.

Keywords: wages, manufacturing, productivity, minimum wage, industrial adjustment, structural transformation, Africa.
